# Matthews Auction OS — Sale Order Optimization

## PURPOSE

Sale Order Optimization converts Catalog Intelligence into a closing sequence designed to maximize:

- Total auction performance
- Bidder retention
- Exposure of important lots
- Competition between buyers
- Category performance
- Buyer satisfaction
- Overall pacing of the auction

The goal is not simply to rearrange lots. The system should deliberately shape the auction so strong merchandise appears at useful times, related buyers can follow the items they care about, and weaker stretches do not cause bidders to disengage.

Sale Order Optimization must use the intelligence already created during Catalog Intelligence rather than re-analyzing the auction from scratch.

---

# 1. CORE PRINCIPLE

There is no universal best sale order.

The correct strategy depends on:

- Auction type
- Lot count
- Total desired closing duration
- Closing rate
- Buyer groups
- Merchandise mix
- Number and strength of anchor lots
- Collections and runs
- Substitutable merchandise
- Historical performance
- Soft-close or extension rules

Do not assume every auction should close at the same rate or over the same number of hours.

---

# 2. DETERMINE AUCTION TIMING FIRST

Before setting sale order, establish the intended auction duration.

When available, identify:

- Total lot count
- Desired total closing duration
- Lots scheduled to close per minute
- Extension/soft-close rules
- Any special timing constraints

## If duration is specified

Use the lot count and desired duration to determine the approximate closing rate.

Example:

1,200 lots over 4 hours:

1,200 ÷ 240 minutes = 5 lots per minute.

Use the platform's actual supported closing-rate options when known.

## If closing rate is specified

Calculate the approximate total duration.

Example:

600 lots at 4 lots per minute:

600 ÷ 4 = 150 minutes  
Expected duration = approximately 2.5 hours.

## If neither is specified

Recommend a closing duration and rate based on:

- Auction type
- Lot count
- Number of major anchors
- Number of distinct buyer groups
- Amount of similar merchandise
- Need to keep specialized buyers engaged
- Typical buyer behavior
- Value concentration
- Complexity of the catalog

Explain the recommendation briefly before finalizing the sale order.

The user may override the recommendation.

---

# 3. THINK IN AUCTION PERCENTAGES

Do not hard-code rules such as "Hours 2 and 3 are always best."

Instead, think in terms of the auction timeline.

A useful general framework is:

## Opening Phase
Approximately first 10–20%.

Goals:
- Establish activity quickly
- Avoid opening with weak or confusing merchandise
- Give bidders confidence that useful merchandise is coming
- Include enough quality to prevent early disengagement

Do not use every major anchor immediately.

---

## Early-Middle Phase
Approximately 20–40%.

Goals:
- Begin increasing merchandise strength
- Introduce important buyer categories
- Place secondary anchors
- Build momentum

---

## Core Phase
Approximately 40–75%.

This is generally the strongest section of the auction.

Goals:
- Highest concentration of major anchors
- Strong category sequences
- High bidder participation
- Strong value density
- Buyer-group overlap

For a four-hour weekly auction, this often corresponds roughly with Hours 2 and 3.

For a two-hour estate auction, the same principle applies to the middle portion rather than literal hours.

---

## Late Phase
Approximately 75–90%.

Goals:
- Maintain meaningful merchandise
- Avoid a sharp drop after the major anchors
- Give buyers a reason to continue bidding
- Use secondary anchors and strong supporting groups

---

## Final Phase
Approximately last 10%.

Goals:
- Finish with legitimate buyer interest
- Avoid ending with nothing but leftovers
- Use strong but not necessarily top-tier merchandise
- Prevent bidders from mentally checking out too early

Do not automatically place the single best item last.

---

# 4. ANCHOR STRATEGY

Use Catalog Intelligence classifications.

## Major Anchors

Major anchors should generally:

- Be spread throughout the strongest portions of the auction
- Avoid clustering unless a category-specific strategy requires it
- Be surrounded by useful supporting merchandise
- Help maintain bidder attention
- Avoid being buried in weak merchandise
- Avoid all closing within a short time window

When several major anchors appeal to different buyer groups, distribute them to create multiple points of interest.

When several major anchors appeal to the same buyer group, consider enough spacing to avoid budget exhaustion while still keeping that audience engaged.

---

## Secondary Anchors

Use secondary anchors to:

- Bridge gaps between major anchors
- Strengthen the opening and late phases
- Prevent long weak stretches
- Support major buyer categories
- Maintain pacing

Secondary anchors are especially important when an auction has only a few true major anchors.

---

# 5. DO NOT CREATE DEAD ZONES

A dead zone is a long stretch of low-interest merchandise that may cause bidders to leave.

Look for:

- Large runs of ordinary household merchandise
- Repetitive low-value returns
- Minor accessories
- Weak furniture
- Commodity merchandise
- Low-interest miscellaneous lots

Break weak stretches when possible with:

- Secondary anchors
- Traffic drivers
- Strong category lots
- Visually interesting merchandise
- Popular tools
- Recognizable brands
- High-demand practical items

Do not waste every strong item in the core phase if doing so leaves the rest of the auction flat.

---

# 6. BUYER-GROUP STRATEGY

Sale order should reflect how buyers behave.

Identify the major buyer groups from Catalog Intelligence.

Examples may include:

- Contractors
- Mechanics
- Farmers
- Equipment buyers
- Motorcycle buyers
- Woodworkers
- Collectors
- Antique buyers
- Furniture buyers
- Builders
- Resellers
- Homeowners
- Restaurant operators

For each buyer group, consider:

- How many relevant lots exist
- How long that buyer is likely to remain engaged
- Whether the lots should be grouped
- Whether the merchandise competes for the same budget
- Whether other categories overlap with that buyer

Do not scatter highly specialized merchandise so widely that the buyer has to monitor the auction for hours unnecessarily.

Do not put every competing item back-to-back if buyers are likely to purchase only one.

---

# 7. GROUP COMPLEMENTARY ITEMS

Keep merchandise together when buyers benefit from seeing it as a run or collection.

Examples include:

- Chains
- Matching furniture
- Flooring followed by underlayment
- Motorcycle-related merchandise
- Tractor implements
- Welding equipment and accessories
- Shop equipment
- Specialized tool collections
- Related collectibles
- Restaurant equipment systems

Grouping is especially useful when:

- The same buyer likely wants multiple items
- The category is specialized
- Buyers could miss items if they are widely scattered
- The group creates momentum
- Items make more sense together

A collection should not automatically be broken apart merely to distribute value.

---

# 8. SEPARATE SUBSTITUTE MERCHANDISE

Separate similar items when buyers are likely to view them as alternatives.

Examples:

- Similar blenders
- Comparable refrigerators
- Similar generators
- Comparable lawn equipment
- Similar furniture pieces
- Duplicate retail-return merchandise

Reasons to separate substitutes:

- Prevent buyer-budget exhaustion
- Give later buyers another chance
- Avoid depressing competition through oversupply
- Spread desirable merchandise across the auction
- Maintain interest

Use judgment.

Three identical low-value products may remain together if separating them offers no strategic benefit.

Multiple expensive substitute items may need meaningful spacing.

---

# 9. COLLECTIONS AND SPECIALIZED RUNS

When a true collection exists, preserve the experience of that collection.

Examples:

- Estate chains
- Vintage advertising
- Motorcycle collection
- Coin group
- Specialty tools
- Farm implements
- Antique furniture
- Firearm accessories where applicable

Do not spread a specialized collection across an entire auction simply to distribute "good items."

The specialized buyer may not want to follow unrelated merchandise for hours.

Instead:

- Place the collection in a strong auction window
- Use internal pacing within the collection
- Position major pieces strategically within the run
- Keep supporting items close enough to retain the same audience

---

# 10. CATEGORY PACING

Do not create overly repetitive category blocks unless there is a clear reason.

A useful rhythm may be:

Strong category run  
→ different buyer category  
→ anchor  
→ supporting merchandise  
→ another category run

However, intentional blocks are appropriate when:

- Buyers are highly specialized
- The category works as a collection
- Buyers may miss scattered items
- Merchandise is complementary

The goal is deliberate pacing, not arbitrary variety.

---

# 11. WEEKLY RETAIL / STORE-RETURN AUCTIONS

Weekly auctions usually contain high lot volume and repeated product categories.

Priorities include:

- Strong middle portion
- Frequent traffic drivers
- Category balance
- Separation of repeated commodities
- Contractor/building-material buyer retention
- Avoiding long runs of low-value household merchandise
- Spreading high-interest consumer goods
- Strategic handling of multiples

Strong merchandise should generally be concentrated in the central portion while preserving enough quality in the opening and final phases.

If historical Matthews results indicate specific closing periods perform better, use that evidence.

Do not blindly assume current bids or watchers identify the best anchor positions.

---

# 12. ESTATE AUCTIONS

Estate auctions often have fewer lots and more specialized buyer groups.

The system should pay extra attention to:

- Collections
- Tools
- Motorcycles
- Vehicles
- Equipment
- Antiques
- Collectibles
- Shop merchandise
- Furniture groups
- Unique estate items

Estate auctions may benefit from more intentional category runs than weekly returns auctions.

For specialized groups such as motorcycles, chains, or a tool collection:

- Keep enough proximity that buyers can follow the run
- Avoid excessive fragmentation
- Place the run in a strong window
- Use the best item within the run as an internal anchor

Estate auctions may close faster or slower depending on lot count and buyer complexity.

Do not assume the weekly-auction rate.

---

# 13. EQUIPMENT AUCTIONS

Equipment buyers often monitor specific assets rather than every lot.

Priorities include:

- Major equipment spacing
- Attachment relationships
- Buyer budget
- Comparable machines
- Category specialization
- Commercial buyer behavior

Avoid putting several expensive comparable machines immediately together unless there is a strategic reason.

Examples:

A skid steer may be followed by compatible attachments.

Two comparable skid steers may benefit from separation.

A tractor and its related implements may remain relatively close if the same buyer group is likely to want both.

Equipment auctions may require slower pacing than high-volume retail merchandise.

If timing is not specified, recommend an appropriate rate.

---

# 14. BUSINESS LIQUIDATIONS

Understand the operating workflow of the business.

Related systems may need to stay together.

Examples:

- Main machine
- Supporting equipment
- Tooling
- Material handling
- Specialized accessories

Separate substitute major assets when needed, but preserve logical production relationships.

Use major operating equipment as anchors.

---

# 15. SOFT-CLOSE AND EXTENSION RULES

Account for the auction platform's extension rules.

If a bid placed within a specified final window extends a lot:

- Recognize that high-interest lots may remain open longer
- Understand that the actual auction may exceed the scheduled duration
- Avoid assuming all major anchors will finish at exact scheduled times
- Consider bidder workload when several strong lots close near each other

If each lot extends independently, an extension on one lot does not automatically change the scheduled closing time of later lots unless the platform specifically behaves that way.

Do not invent platform behavior.

---

# 16. BIDDER WORKLOAD

Avoid creating unnecessary bidding conflicts.

A buyer interested in several related high-value lots may struggle if they close almost simultaneously.

This is especially important for:

- Equipment
- Vehicles
- Major tools
- Specialized collections
- High-value building materials
- Major furniture sets

Closing pace and lot spacing should give serious buyers a reasonable chance to participate.

---

# 17. USING CURRENT BIDS AND WATCHERS

Current bids and watchers are useful but secondary signals.

They may identify:

- Unexpected interest
- A potentially overlooked anchor
- Strong buyer demand
- A misjudged category

But do not:

- Automatically move the highest current bids into the best positions
- Assume low-current-bid items are weak
- Treat watcher count as value
- Let early bidding completely override item intelligence

The sale order should still reflect underlying merchandise quality and buyer behavior.

---

# 18. HISTORICAL PERFORMANCE

When completed Matthews auction data is available, compare:

- Sale position
- Auction percentage
- Final price
- Retail/value information
- Category
- Buyer group
- Anchor designation
- Intentional vs lot-number sequencing
- Grouped vs separated merchandise

Historical results should be used to test hypotheses such as:

- Whether intentional sequencing improves total performance
- Whether middle-auction placement benefits anchors
- Whether category blocks help specialized buyers
- Whether substitute separation improves results

Do not claim causation from small samples.

Treat limited data as directional evidence.

---

# 19. RANDOMNESS SHOULD BE CONTROLLED

Do not simply randomize sale order.

Intentional variation is useful, but every major placement decision should have a reason.

Examples:

Good reason:
"Separate these three similar generators so each reaches a fresh bidder window."

Poor reason:
"Move them randomly to create variety."

Randomness may be used only within low-priority merchandise where exact placement has little strategic consequence.

---

# 20. SALE ORDER CONSTRUCTION PROCESS

When building a full sale order, use this sequence:

## Step 1 — Establish timing
Determine duration, closing rate, and extension rules.

## Step 2 — Map auction phases
Define opening, early-middle, core, late, and final windows.

## Step 3 — Place major anchors
Distribute major anchors across the strongest windows.

## Step 4 — Place buyer-group runs
Position important collections and specialized categories.

## Step 5 — Add secondary anchors
Prevent weak gaps.

## Step 6 — Position complementary lots
Keep logical relationships intact.

## Step 7 — Separate substitutes
Create useful spacing for competing merchandise.

## Step 8 — Fill remaining lots
Distribute ordinary merchandise without creating dead zones.

## Step 9 — Review buyer experience
Check whether specialized buyers can reasonably follow their categories.

## Step 10 — Validate timing
Ensure the sequence fits the intended duration.

---

# 21. FINAL VALIDATION

Before producing the final set-lots file, check:

- Every lot is included exactly once unless explicitly excluded
- No duplicate sale-order numbers
- No missing required sale-order values
- Major anchors are intentionally placed
- Strong merchandise is not accidentally concentrated too tightly
- No major buyer category is unnecessarily scattered
- Collections are handled deliberately
- Substitute items have appropriate spacing
- Opening is not excessively weak
- Final portion retains meaningful merchandise
- Intended duration and closing rate are consistent
- Spreadsheet structure is preserved
- Required columns remain intact

---

# 22. SET-LOTS SPREADSHEET RULES

When a set-lots template is provided:

- Preserve existing required columns
- Do not rename columns unless requested
- Do not delete supporting data unnecessarily
- Populate the Sale Order field correctly
- Keep lot identifiers unchanged
- Do not alter lot numbers or control numbers
- Validate that each lot maps correctly between source and template

If the template contains only a subset of source data, use reliable identifiers to match records.

---

# 23. EXPLAINING THE STRATEGY

Do not overwhelm the user with explanations for every ordinary lot.

When useful, summarize:

- Recommended auction duration
- Closing rate
- Strongest auction window
- Major anchors
- Major buyer groups
- Important grouped runs
- Important separated substitutes
- Any unusual strategy choices

Then provide the finished file.

The operational output matters more than a long narrative.

---

# 24. COMMON FAILURE MODES

Avoid these mistakes:

## Hard-coding a four-hour auction
Different auction types and lot counts require different timing.

## Putting all best items together
This can exhaust buyers and create weak stretches elsewhere.

## Spreading every strong item evenly
Some concentration in the strongest auction phase is desirable.

## Breaking specialized collections apart
This can cause the intended buyers to miss merchandise.

## Grouping every similar item
Substitutes may perform better with spacing.

## Overweighting current bids
Early bids do not equal eventual importance.

## Ignoring the final portion
Buyers may leave if the auction visibly collapses in quality.

## Putting all weak merchandise first
A poor opening can reduce engagement before the auction reaches its strongest items.

## Ignoring buyer workload
Serious bidders should not be forced to compete on several related major lots at nearly the same second.

---

# 25. DECISION STANDARD

Every meaningful sale-order decision should answer at least one of these questions:

- Does this placement improve bidder retention?
- Does it protect an important lot?
- Does it help the right buyer find related merchandise?
- Does it prevent substitute items from competing with each other too aggressively?
- Does it strengthen a weak portion of the auction?
- Does it support a major buyer group?
- Does it improve the overall rhythm of the auction?

If the placement has no meaningful strategic effect, do not over-optimize it.

Focus effort where sequencing can actually change results.
